# The Evolution of Reinforcement Learning in Automated Theorem Proving: From Neural Foundations to Reasoning-Centric Synthesis

Automated theorem proving (ATP) represents one of the most rigorous challenges in artificial intelligence, necessitating a transition from simple pattern recognition to complex, multi-step logical reasoning. While traditional symbolic methods provided the initial framework for formal logic, they often struggled with the combinatorial explosion inherent in vast search spaces. The integration of machine learning has fundamentally altered this landscape, beginning with the Foundations of Neural Theorem Proving and Premise Selection, which sought to optimize the selection of relevant axioms and lemmas. Solving this problem is critical not only for the advancement of pure mathematics but also for the formal verification of software and hardware systems, where absolute correctness is a prerequisite for safety and reliability.

The trajectory of the field has evolved from these early predictive models toward sophisticated systems governed by Reinforcement Learning and Preference Alignment for Reasoning. As the field has matured, the emergence of Scaling Laws and Large Language Models in Mathematics has shifted the focus toward leveraging massive datasets to capture mathematical intuition. This transition has been supported by significant Methodological Advances, including the use of Synthetic Data, Autoformalization, and Verifiers to bridge the gap between informal human reasoning and formal machine-readable logic. This review traces this progression from its neural origins to the current Frontier of Reasoning-Centric Models and RL-Driven Synthesis, ultimately examining how the field is moving toward Evaluating Mathematical Intelligence through benchmarks designed for Live Discovery.

## Foundations of Neural Theorem Proving and Premise Selection

Early efforts in automated theorem proving (ATP) focused on integrating reasoning services with formal authoring environments. For instance, the development of the Automated Reasoning for Mizar (Miz) service established a method for soundly translating the Mizar language into first-order ATP systems to provide proof assistance and problem generation, as demonstrated in the formalization of simplicial complexes [arXiv:1109.0616]. As formal mathematical libraries grew, the challenge shifted toward managing the vast number of available lemmas through premise selection.

A significant methodological advancement was the two-phase approach introduced to improve theorem proving in large libraries by combining minimal dependency analysis of formal proofs with a kernel-based multi-output ranking (MOR) algorithm [arXiv:1108.3446]. This work expanded the available benchmarks significantly, moving from the 252-problem MPTP Challenge to a 2078-problem dataset [arXiv:1108.3446]. Parallel to premise selection, research also addressed "lemma mining"—the estimation of lemma usefulness for proving further theorems. By evaluating various scenarios ranging from expert-evaluation to "cheating ATP" scenarios using full proof graphs, researchers established criteria for identifying informative lemmas within the HOL Light and Flyspeck libraries [arXiv:1402.3578].

The transition from kernel methods to deep learning marked a pivotal shift in the field. DeepMath introduced a two-stage deep learning approach that utilized separate embedding networks for conjecture and axiom sequences to predict the probability of an axiom's relevance [arXiv:1606.04442]. Unlike previous methods, this approach avoided hand-engineered features and improved the generalization of infrequent symbols through function symbol embeddings, though it achieved its best results when ensembled with traditional feature-based methods [arXiv:1606.04442]. Similarly, deep learning was applied to guide clause selection heuristics in the E prover; by using an embedding and combiner network to score clauses, the model could accurately predict their utility in a final proof [arXiv:1701.06972]. However, this approach was limited by its simplicity, as inputs were restricted to the negated conjecture and a single clause rather than the entire set of processed clauses [arXiv:1701.06972].

Recent research has expanded these foundations into neural translation and interactive reinforcement learning. Neural machine translation (NMT) models have been used to translate informal LaTeX mathematical text into the formal Mizar language, achieving perfect translation on 65.73% of test pairs [arXiv:1805.06502]. Finally, the field has moved toward interactive environments like HOList, which provides a reinforcement learning framework for predicting tactics in higher-order logic [arXiv:1904.03241], and CoqGym, which enables models like ASTactic to generate tactics as abstract syntax trees [arXiv:1905.09381]. While HOList facilitates interaction with HOL Light, it currently lacks support for tactics that require terms as arguments [arXiv:1904.03241].

## Reinforcement Learning and Preference Alignment for Reasoning

The application of reinforcement learning (RL) to formal reasoning has evolved from guiding search heuristics to the complex alignment of large language models (LLMs) with human or AI preferences. Early efforts in this domain focused on automating the search process itself; for instance, arXiv:1805.07563 introduced a theorem proving algorithm that replaced traditional domain heuristics with RL-guided policy and value guidance within a connection tableau setting, utilizing Monte-Carlo simulations to navigate the Miz40 and M2k datasets.

As the field shifted toward LLMs, the focus transitioned from search guidance to alignment. A foundational milestone in this transition is the use of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) to fine-tune models to be helpful and harmless while maintaining specialized skills like Python coding (arXiv:2204.05862). This process typically relies on the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm (arXiv:1707.06347), which enables multiple epochs of minibatch updates per data sample by optimizing a surrogate objective function. While PPO is a standard for balancing sample complexity and simplicity (arXiv:1707.06347), its implementation in LLMs is complex. Recent analyses have sought to stabilize this process, such as the proposal of the PPO-max algorithm for more stable training (arXiv:2307.04964).

However, the complexity of PPO has prompted the development of more streamlined alternatives. RRHF (Rank Responses to Align Language Models with Human Feedback) offers a simpler alternative to PPO by extending supervised fine-tuning to rank responses, achieving comparable performance on the Anthropic Helpful and Harmless dataset while requiring fewer models and hyperparameters (arXiv:2304.05302). Similarly, RLOO introduces a REINFORCE-style optimization designed to reduce complexity and increase robustness to noise and KL penalty sensitivity compared to PPO (arXiv:2402.14740).

Theoretical advancements have also sought to formalize these alignment methods. arXiv:2301.11270 provides a framework unifying RLHF with max-entropy IRL, demonstrating that a "pessimistic" Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE) can outperform standard MLE when the true reward is linear. Beyond human feedback, the field has moved toward Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF). Research shows that using an off-the-shelf LLM to annotate preferences via structured prompting can achieve performance comparable or even superior to RLHF across summarization and dialogue tasks (arXiv:2309.00267). Despite these advances, a notable limitation in current RLHF methodologies is the lack of explicit focus on honesty and truthfulness, suggesting that further specialized techniques may be required to ensure model veracity (arXiv:2204.05862).

## Scaling Laws and Large Language Models in Mathematics

The evolution of mathematical reasoning in large language models (LLMs) has been driven by a shift from general-purpose scaling to specialized architectural and data-centric refinements. Early foundational work established that scaling model size, dataset size, and training length allows models to perform tasks via in-context learning without task-specific fine-tuning, with performance following a predictable power-law [arXiv:2005.14165]. This relationship is further elucidated by theoretical frameworks that identify four distinct scaling regimes—variance-limited and resolution-limited for both model and dataset sizes—which explain how interpolation exponents align with theoretical predictions [arXiv:2102.06701].

As models scaled, research shifted toward eliciting latent reasoning capabilities. A pivotal advancement was the introduction of chain-of-thought prompting, which significantly improves performance on complex arithmetic and symbolic reasoning tasks by providing intermediate reasoning steps as exemplars [arXiv:2201.11903]. While general scaling provides the foundation, recent literature suggests that mathematical expertise requires more than just increasing parameter counts. For instance, investigations into mathematical reasoning scaling relationships indicate that pre-training loss serves as a superior performance indicator compared to mere parameter count [arXiv:2308.01825]. This is evidenced by the efficacy of rejection sampling from multiple models (RFT); for example, applying RFT to a LLaMA-7B model increased accuracy to 49.3, a 13.4-point improvement over standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) [arXiv:2308.01825].

Current research frontiers focus on domain-specific specialization and self-improvement pipelines. Llemma demonstrates the power of continued pre-training, utilizing a 55B-token mixture of scientific papers and mathematical code to outperform existing open base models on the MATH benchmark [arXiv:2310.10631]. This trajectory toward expertise is further advanced by the Qwen2.5-Math series, which adopts a "self-improvement" philosophy integrated across pre-training, post-training, and inference [arXiv:2409.12122]. Unlike earlier models that relied primarily on prompting [arXiv:2201.11903], Qwen2.5-Math incorporates a reward model developed through massive sampling and introduces Tool-Integrated Reasoning (TIR) via a Python Interpreter [arXiv:2409.12122]. The results of this integrated approach are substantial, with the 72B model achieving nearly 90 points on the MATH benchmark and even the smaller 1.5B model reaching approximately 80 points [arXiv:2409.12122], suggesting that specialized data mixtures and self-improvement loops may be more critical for mathematical mastery than the raw scaling laws observed in general language modeling [arXiv:2005.14165].

## Methodological Advances: Synthetic Data, Autoformalization, and Verifiers

Recent methodological shifts in automated theorem proving have transitioned from simple supervised learning toward sophisticated pipelines involving synthetic data generation, autoformalization, and closed-loop verification. A central challenge in this domain is the scarcity of high-quality formal proofs, a gap addressed by various bootstrapping and augmentation strategies. MetaMath addresses this by proposing MetaMathQA, a dataset created through question bootstrapping—rewriting questions from multiple perspectives—and answer augmentation via few-shot chain-of-thought prompting to generate diverse reasoning paths (arXiv:2309.12284). Similarly, the TP-as-a-Judge framework introduces Reinforcement Learning with Theorem Prover Feedback (RLTPF) to validate intermediate reasoning steps in synthetic data, achieving competitive performance on benchmarks like AIME 2024 despite using significantly smaller datasets than traditional fine-tuning methods (arXiv:2502.13137).

The bridge between informal mathematical reasoning and formal verification is primarily managed through autoformalization. ProofNet introduces a benchmark specifically for undergraduate-level mathematics, utilizing distilled backtranslation and embedding database searches to train its proofGPT models (arXiv:2302.12433). However, ProofNet is constrained by strict inclusion criteria, requiring problems to be self-contained and free of nonstandard notation, which may limit its generalizability to more complex mathematical structures. This necessity for a more robust approach is echoed in recent efforts to establish a common framework for autoformalization based on semantic equivalence to abstract away domain-specific complexities (arXiv:2509.09810).

To facilitate these advancements, infrastructure that enables interaction with formal environments is critical. LeanDojo provides an open-source toolkit that allows for programmatic interaction with Lean and extracts runtime intermediate states from repositories like mathlib, lowering the barrier to entry for training retrieval-augmented models (arXiv:2306.15626). This interaction between language models and formal environments is further refined in the Draft, Sketch, and Prove (DSP) method, which uses a three-stage pipeline to leverage informal proofs as "sketches" to guide automated provers (arXiv:2210.12283).

The current research frontier integrates these components into high-performance reinforcement learning loops. DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5 represents a significant milestone by combining formal language specialization with Reinforcement Learning from Proof Assistant Feedback (RLPAF) and Monte-Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), achieving state-of-the-art pass rates on ProofNet (arXiv:2408.08152). This trend toward scalable synthesis is furthered by recent work proposing stepwise LLM-based provers that utilize diverse tactic synthesis and adaptive beam sizes during tree search to improve exploration (arXiv:2505.12031). While these methods show promise, the field continues to grapple with the fact that autoformalization remains inherently error-prone (arXiv:2502.13137).

## The Frontier of Reasoning-Centric Models and RL-Driven Synthesis

The current research frontier in automated theorem proving (ATP) is characterized by a transition from reactive tactic generation toward high-level strategic reasoning and large-scale reinforcement learning (RL) optimization. Recent developments suggest that the field is moving beyond simple feedback loops to embrace "reasoning-centric" architectures that integrate planning, reflection, and agentic workflows.

A primary driver of recent progress is the application of advanced RL frameworks to optimize Large Language Models (LLMs) for formal reasoning. For instance, arXiv:2502.08908 utilizes a GRPO-based framework via TRL to iteratively optimize pretrained LLMs by rolling out next tactics and comparing them against expected ones, incorporating GPT-4o-generated Chain-of-Thought (CoT) signals to enhance the training dataset. Similarly, arXiv:2504.06122 investigates posttraining scaling, achieving a 59.8% (pass@32) accuracy on MiniF2F-test by applying a scaling framework to 7B parameter models using a Qwen2.5-based formalizer. This highlights a trend where scaling the posttraining phase—rather than just the model size—is critical for aligning formal reasoning with natural language capabilities.

The literature also reveals a divergence in how models handle informal versus formal reasoning. While arXiv:2502.08908 and arXiv:2504.06122 focus on formalizing and proving within Lean, arXiv:2505.23754 introduces DeepTheorem, which leverages an "RL-Zero" strategy specifically tailored for informal theorem proving using a large-scale dataset of 121K IMO-level theorems. This distinction is vital, as it addresses the gap between natural language mathematical intuition and formal verification.

A significant methodological leap is observed in the "Seed-Prover" lineage. Seed-Prover (arXiv:2507.23726) introduces a lemma-style whole-proof reasoning model that uses iterative refinement via Lean feedback and self-summarization, achieving high performance on IMO 2025 problems. This evolves into Seed-Prover 1.5 (arXiv:2512.17260), which employs large-scale agentic RL and an efficient test-time scaling (TTS) workflow. By utilizing an incremental caching mechanism for compiled lemmas, Seed-Prover 1.5 manages context window efficiency to master undergraduate and PhD-level problems, such as the 2025 Putnam Competition.

Finally, the frontier is shifting toward high-level cognitive strategies. ReCent-Prover (arXiv:2604.19558) represents a departure from purely reactive tactic generation, introducing "validation with reflection" and "retrieval with planning." This reasoning-centric approach allows for LLM-based self-critique and semantic retrieval based on natural-language proof plans. While ReCent-Prover demonstrates a 22.58% relative improvement over previous state-of-the-art agents on the CoqStoq benchmark, its practical utility is potentially constrained by the computational overhead of additional LLM calls for reflection—a weakness not fully addressed in its current analysis. This trajectory suggests that the future of ATP lies in the synergy between neuro-symbolic coordination, such as the DSP+ framework (arXiv:2506.11487), and sophisticated agentic planning.

## Evaluating Mathematical Intelligence: From Benchmarks to Live Discovery

As reinforcement learning for theorem proving shifts from competition-style mathematics toward genuine scientific discovery, the field has encountered a critical bottleneck: the inadequacy of static benchmarks. Traditional datasets often fail to capture the complexity of research-level reasoning, leading to a significant gap between model performance on standard tasks and their utility in real-world mathematical research. Recent literature suggests a transition toward "living" benchmarks that evolve alongside human mathematical progress.

A primary challenge in current evaluation is the lack of formal rigor and the risk of data contamination. To address this, RLMEval introduces a suite specifically for research-level neural theorem proving and autoformalization, utilizing 613 theorems from six Lean Blueprint projects [arXiv:2510.25427]. This approach highlights a stark reality: the best-performing models achieved only a 10.3% pass rate, underscoring that existing benchmarks do not adequately reflect realistic research settings [arXiv:2510.25427]. Similarly, Riemann-Bench targets "moonshot mathematics" by evaluating frontier models on 25 PhD-level problems authored by advanced mathematicians [arXiv:2604.06802]. By providing models with full access to coding tools and search, Riemann-Bench reveals that even advanced agents struggle, with all evaluated models scoring below a 10% pass rate [arXiv:2604.06802].

To combat the obsolescence of static datasets, several frameworks propose automated pipelines for continuous evaluation. EternalMath establishes a theorem-grounded pipeline that transforms recent peer-reviewed literature into executable, verifiable reasoning tasks [arXiv:2601.01400]. This "living benchmark" demonstrates that frontier-level reasoning remains far from saturated [arXiv:2601.01400]. A related approach, LemmaBench, utilizes a language-model-based pipeline to extract and rewrite lemmas from arXiv into self-contained statements, ensuring that implicit assumptions are identified and made explicit [arXiv:2602.24173]. While EternalMath focuses on the transformation of literature into tasks, LemmaBench specifically optimizes the structural integrity of the mathematical statements themselves [arXiv:2602.24173].

Other methodologies focus on specialized formats to prevent contamination. LiveMathematicianBench employs a seven-stage pipeline to generate research-level multiple-choice questions (MCQs) from recent arXiv papers, specifically selecting content released after model knowledge cutoffs [arXiv:2604.01754]. While effective for testing logical forms like implication and existence, this method is distinct from the more comprehensive MathArena platform. MathArena moves beyond individual benchmarks to provide a continuously maintained evaluation ecosystem that aggregates diverse protocols, including proof-based competitions and formal Lean generation [arXiv:2605.00674]. While specialized benchmarks like PRL-Bench extend these evaluative principles to frontier physics research—focusing on theoretical derivation and procedural complexity [arXiv:2604.15411]—the field is clearly moving toward integrated, evolving platforms like MathArena to measure the true trajectory of mathematical intelligence [arXiv:2605.00674].

## Tensions and Open Problems

A fundamental tension persists regarding the appropriate level of abstraction for mathematical evaluation. On one hand, early benchmarks focused on high-school and undergraduate-level quantitative reasoning, such as the MATH dataset (arXiv:2103.03874), which emphasizes procedural correctness and step-by-step verification (arXiv:2110.14168). However, recent critiques argue that these datasets fail to capture the depth of professional mathematics, suggesting they suffer from data contamination and lack the complexity required for true reasoning. This has led to the development of more rigorous, "frontier" benchmarks like FrontierMath (arXiv:2411.04872), which targets advanced mathematical problems that current models struggle to solve. This debate has further intensified with the emergence of "living" benchmarks like EternalMath (arXiv:2601.01400), which argues that static datasets are inherently flawed and must be continuously updated to prevent models from simply memorizing solutions, a critique leveled against previous iterative bootstrapping methods like MetaMath (arXiv:2309.12284) and even specialized scaling efforts like DeepSeekMath (arXiv:2402.03300).

Another critical disagreement concerns the methodology of formalization and the role of Large Language Models (LLMs) in theorem proving. One school of thought emphasizes the power of few-shot learning and general-purpose reasoning, as seen in the foundational observations of arXiv:2005.14165, which suggests that scale alone can unlock significant reasoning capabilities. This view is challenged by the necessity of specialized, neuro-symbolic integration; for instance, the LeanDojo framework (arXiv:2306.15626) argues that general-purpose models like GPT-4 (arXiv:2303.08774) are insufficient for formal verification without tight integration with interactive theorem provers (ITPs) and retrieval-augmented strategies. Furthermore, while some researchers advocate for purely generative approaches to theorem proving (arXiv:2009.03393), others argue that these methods lack the structural rigor provided by hybrid systems that combine language modeling with automated tactic application (arXiv:2205.10893). This tension extends to the very nature of autoformalization, where the gap between natural language undergraduate mathematics (arXiv:2206.14858) and formal machine-readable proofs (arXiv:2302.12433) remains a primary bottleneck for scaling mathematical AI.

Finally, there is an ongoing debate regarding the scaling laws of mathematical reasoning. While some studies suggest that increasing model scale and few-shot capabilities provides a predictable path to improved performance (arXiv:2308.01825), others point to a "reasoning ceiling" where standard scaling fails to translate into true logical depth. This has prompted a shift toward more complex training objectives, such as the move from simple next-token prediction to reward-ranked fine-tuning (arXiv:2304.06767) and self-supervised skip-tree training (arXiv:2006.04757), which aim to better capture the hierarchical structure of mathematical thought.

### Outlook

The field is rapidly moving toward a "Measure-Explain-Test-Improve" paradigm (arXiv:2605.02233) that transcends static evaluation in favor of dynamic, live benchmarking (arXiv:2602.24173). The frontier is no longer just about solving math word problems, but about achieving "verified discovery"—the ability for AI to autonomously propose and formally prove new mathematical or physical conjectures (arXiv:2606.08728, arXiv:2601.15737). As we move toward reasoning-centric architectures (arXiv:2604.19558), the integration of unified neural scaling laws (arXiv:2605.26248) with the rigorous constraints of interactive theorem provers will likely define the next generation of mathematical intelligence.
